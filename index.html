Where the terrain comes from

How it gets onto your screen

  1. Download NASA's LROC NAC digital terrain model of the Chandrayaan-3 landing area (a 488 MB GeoTIFF, 7668 × 15895 cells, 3 m each).
  2. Read the GeoTIFF tags directly. The projection is south polar stereographic on a 1737.4 km sphere. Check that the corners match the product's own latitude and longitude limits.
  3. Convert the published landing point (69.373°S, 32.319°E) to a cell and cut a 1024 × 1024 window around it, about 3.07 km on a side.
  4. Fill the few no-data cells from their neighbours, then store heights as 16-bit numbers (2 mm steps). The page loads 2 MB.
  5. In your browser: slopes come from height differences over 12 m. Hillshade is lit from low in the sky. Rover routes use Dijkstra's algorithm over 16 directions, written for this project.
  6. The lander flies in the vertical plane along the approach line you pick. Its ground is the terrain profile under that line.

What this is not

  • Not a mission planning tool. The physics is simplified and the numbers are not ISRO's.
  • Not the real terrain after landing. The terrain model was built from images taken before the landing. Its 3 m cells cannot show rocks or small craters, which are what really decide a landing.
  • Not Pragyan's route. The rover tab shows what a rover with an assumed slope limit could reach. It does not replay what the real rover did.
  • Scoring limits for touchdown speed and ground slope come from ISRO's published Chandrayaan-3 specification.
  • Similar real-terrain simulators exist. This one differs by showing the data pipeline and letting you compare rover routes.
